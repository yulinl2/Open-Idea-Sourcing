# Weighted Conformal Prediction for Counterfactual Outcomes Under Treatment Assignment

Understanding treatment heterogeneity requires moving beyond average effects to quantify uncertainty about individual-level causal effects. While machine learning methods can estimate conditional average treatment effects (CATE), they typically provide poor uncertainty quantification, particularly problematic in high-stakes decision-making contexts like medicine and policy. A fundamental challenge is that we never observe both potential outcomes for any individual, and the distribution of covariates differs between treated and control groups even in randomized experiments, creating a covariate shift problem that invalidates standard prediction methods for counterfactual inference.

We propose a novel approach that extends conformal prediction to construct distribution-free prediction intervals for counterfactual outcomes by explicitly accounting for the treatment assignment mechanism. Our method leverages the known propensity scores in randomized experiments to reweight conformal scores, creating a weighted exchangeability structure that enables valid inference despite covariate shift. Specifically, we construct prediction intervals for the potential outcome Y(1-T) (the counterfactual outcome) for individuals who received treatment T by weighting the nonconformity scores of control subjects by their inverse propensity of receiving the observed treatment, and vice versa.

The key insight is that while standard conformal prediction fails under covariate shift, the treatment assignment mechanism provides the necessary likelihood ratios to correct for this shift. For a subject with covariates X who received treatment T, we construct intervals for their counterfactual outcome Y(1-T) using a weighted quantile of residuals from subjects who actually received treatment 1-T, with weights proportional to π(X)/(1-π(X)) where π(X) is the propensity score.

Our approach achieves exact finite-sample coverage guarantees for randomized experiments without requiring parametric assumptions about the outcome model or error distributions. The method remains valid under misspecification of either the outcome model or the propensity score model (but not both), providing robustness against model misspecification. By first solving the fundamental problem of counterfactual prediction intervals for study subjects, our framework establishes the foundation for subsequent extensions to new populations and individual treatment effect inference, addressing a critical gap in causal inference methodology for personalized decision-making.
